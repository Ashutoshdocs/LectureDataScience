Six ways to sort things into groups

Regression predicts a number. Classification predicts a category: spam or not spam, approve or reject, cat or dog. Every classifier is really drawing a boundary on a map. They differ in what shape of boundary they can draw.

Step 1 · The foundation

Logistic regression: a straight line plus a probability

Despite the name, it's a classifier. It computes a score like linear regression, then squashes it into a probability between 0 and 1.

z = w₀ + w₁·x₁ + w₂·x₂  →  P(B) = 1 / (1 + e−z)
  1. The sigmoid turns any score z into a probability. A big positive z means "almost surely B"; z = 0 means 50/50.
  2. Training measures log loss: confident wrong answers are punished heavily. Gradient descent nudges the weights to reduce it, epoch by epoch.
  3. Pick a threshold (usually 0.5). Probability above it → class B. The boundary is where P = threshold, and it's always a straight line.
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
clf = LogisticRegression().fit(X, y)
clf.predict_proba(X_new)   # probabilities, not just labels

Step 2 · Learning by example

K-nearest neighbours: you are who you live near

KNN doesn't learn a formula at all. It memorises the training points, and to classify a new one it looks at the k closest and takes a vote.

  1. Store every training point. That's the whole "training" step, which is why KNN is called a lazy learner.
  2. For a new point, measure the distance (usually straight-line, Euclidean) to every stored point.
  3. Take the k nearest and let them vote. The majority class wins. The share of votes doubles as a probability.
distance = √((x₁ − a₁)² + (x₂ − a₂)²)

Choosing k: k = 1 follows every stray point (overfitting, jagged islands). A large k smooths everything out and can blur real detail (underfitting). Odd k avoids ties with two classes.

Scale your features. If one feature is in rupees (0–1,000,000) and another in years (0–40), distance is decided almost entirely by rupees. Standardise first. KNN also gets slow on big datasets and weak with many features.
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5)

Step 3 · Twenty questions

Decision tree classifier: split until the boxes are pure

A tree asks yes/no questions like "is x₁ < 4.2?" and keeps splitting the map until each box is mostly one class.

  1. Measure how mixed a group is with Gini impurity. A group that's all A scores 0; a 50/50 mix scores 0.5.
  2. Try every possible split on every feature. Keep the one that lowers the weighted impurity the most. (Entropy / information gain is the other common measure.)
  3. Repeat inside each half until a max depth or the boxes are pure. Each leaf predicts its majority class.
Gini = 1 − (pA² + pB²)

Splits are always on one feature at a time, so the boundary is made of horizontal and vertical edges: boxes. Deep trees carve tiny boxes around single points, which is overfitting.

from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=4, criterion="gini")

Step 4 · Wisdom of the crowd

Random forest classifier: many trees vote

One deep tree is jumpy. Grow many different trees and let them vote. Their individual mistakes cancel out.

  1. Bootstrap: each tree trains on its own random sample of the rows, drawn with replacement.
  2. Random features: at every split, a tree may only look at a random subset of the features (here: 1 of the 2). This keeps trees different from each other.
  3. Vote: each tree gives its answer and the majority wins. (scikit-learn averages the trees' probabilities, which is nearly the same.)

Step 5 · The widest street

Support vector machine: maximise the margin

Many lines can separate two groups. SVM picks the one with the widest empty street between them. Only the points on the edge of the street matter.

  1. Find the boundary that keeps the biggest gap (the margin) to the nearest points of each class.
  2. The points sitting on or inside the margin are the support vectors. Move any other point and the boundary doesn't change.
  3. C sets how strict it is. Small C: wide street, a few points allowed inside (soft margin). Large C: narrow street, tries hard to classify every point.
  4. Kernel trick: the RBF kernel lets SVM draw curves by implicitly mapping points into a higher-dimensional space where a straight cut works. γ sets how wiggly it can get.
Practical notes: always scale features. SVMs shine on small-to-medium datasets with clear margins, but training slows down sharply past ~100k rows, and they don't output probabilities natively.
from sklearn.svm import SVC
SVC(kernel="rbf", C=1.0, gamma="scale")

Step 6 · Thinking in probabilities

Naive Bayes: which class best explains what I see?

Naive Bayes learns what each class typically looks like, then asks: "given these values, which class is more likely to have produced them?"

P(class | x₁, x₂) ∝ P(class) × P(x₁ | class) × P(x₂ | class)
  1. Prior P(class): how common each class is in training.
  2. Likelihood: for each class and each feature, fit a simple distribution. Gaussian NB uses a bell curve (mean and spread). The ellipses on the map show them.
  3. The naive part: assume features are independent within a class, so you can just multiply their likelihoods. Rarely true, but it often works anyway.
  4. Multiply, compare, pick the bigger. Click the map to see the arithmetic.

Extremely fast, works with little data, and is a classic for text (Multinomial NB on word counts powers many spam filters). Its probabilities tend to be overconfident.

from sklearn.naive_bayes import GaussianNB, MultinomialNB
GaussianNB()        # numeric features
MultinomialNB()     # word counts
