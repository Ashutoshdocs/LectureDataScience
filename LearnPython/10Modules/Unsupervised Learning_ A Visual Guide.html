Step 1 · The idea

Clustering: put similar things together

A cluster is a group of points that are more similar to each other than to points in other groups. "Similar" almost always means close together by some distance.

Supervised

Points arrive already coloured. The model learns to colour new points the same way.

Unsupervised

Points arrive grey. The model decides how many groups there are and where they are.

Three ways to define a "group"

Centroid-based

Each group has a centre; points join the nearest centre. K-Means. Fast, but assumes round blobs.

Connectivity-based

Repeatedly merge the two closest groups, building a family tree. Hierarchical. You choose where to cut.

Density-based

A group is a crowded region; sparse points are noise. DBSCAN. Any shape, finds outliers.

Where it's used

Customer segments
Group shoppers by spend, frequency and categories for targeted offers.

Anomaly detection
Points that fit no cluster: fraud, faulty sensors, intrusions.

Organising content
Group news articles, support tickets or photos by topic.

Image compression
Replace millions of colours with the k most typical ones.

How do you know it worked, with no labels? There's no "accuracy". Instead you check internal scores: inertia (how tight clusters are, lower is better) and the silhouette score (−1 to 1: are points closer to their own cluster than to the next one?). And you check whether the groups make sense to a human expert.
Step 2 · Centroid-based

K-Means: pick centres, assign, move, repeat

You choose k, the number of clusters. K-Means then alternates two simple moves until nothing changes. Step through it yourself.

  1. Initialise: drop k centres (centroids) somewhere. Random points from the data, or the smarter k-means++, which spreads them out.
  2. Assign: every point joins its nearest centroid. The shaded areas show each centroid's territory.
  3. Update: move each centroid to the average position of its points.
  4. Repeat 2–3 until no point changes cluster. Each round lowers the inertia: the total squared distance from points to their centroid.
inertia = Σ (distance from each point to its centroid)²

Choosing k: the elbow method. Inertia always falls as k grows, so look for the bend where adding clusters stops helping much.

from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
km = KMeans(n_clusters=3, init="k-means++", n_init=10).fit(X)
km.labels_, km.cluster_centers_, km.inertia_

Step 3 · Connectivity-based

Hierarchical clustering: build a family tree, then cut it

Start with every point as its own cluster. Repeatedly merge the two closest clusters until only one is left. The record of merges is a tree called a dendrogram.

  1. Every point starts alone: n clusters.
  2. Find the two closest clusters and merge them. Record the distance at which they merged: that's the height of the join in the dendrogram.
  3. Repeat until everything is one cluster. This bottom-up version is agglomerative (top-down splitting is called divisive).
  4. Cut the tree with a horizontal line. Every branch hanging below the line is one cluster. Tall gaps in the tree hint at a natural number of clusters.

Linkage decides what "distance between two clusters" means:

  • Single: closest pair of points. Follows chains and curves, but one bridge point can glue groups together.
  • Complete: farthest pair. Compact, evenly sized groups.
  • Average: average of all pairs. A middle ground.
  • Ward: merge whichever pair increases total variance least. Similar spirit to K-Means; the usual default.
from sklearn.cluster import AgglomerativeClustering
AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=3, linkage="ward")

Trade-off: you don't have to choose k in advance, and the tree itself is informative. But it compares every pair of points, so memory and time grow fast. It's comfortable up to roughly ten thousand rows, not millions. Merges are also final: an early mistake is never undone.
Step 4 · Density-based

DBSCAN: clusters are crowds, loners are noise

DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) doesn't ask for k. It grows clusters through crowded regions and labels isolated points as noise.

Two settings: ε (eps), the radius of a point's neighbourhood, and minPts, how many points (itself included) must be inside it to count as "crowded".

  1. Core point: has at least minPts points within ε. Drawn large.
  2. Border point: not crowded itself, but within ε of a core point. Joins that cluster. Drawn small.
  3. Noise: neither. Belongs to no cluster. Drawn as a grey ×.
  4. Start at any core point and keep hopping to core points within ε of each other. Everything reachable is one cluster. Repeat for unvisited core points.

Because clusters grow by chaining neighbourhoods, they can take any shape: moons, rings, snakes. Click any point on the map to see its neighbourhood.

Weak spot: one ε for the whole map. If some clusters are dense and others sparse, no single ε fits both. Pick ε from a k-distance plot, or try HDBSCAN, which adapts to varying density.
from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN
db = DBSCAN(eps=0.5, min_samples=5).fit(X)

Step 5 · The other half

Dimensionality reduction: fewer columns, same story

Real datasets can have hundreds of columns (dimensions). Many are redundant or noisy. Dimensionality reduction compresses them into a few new columns that keep most of the information.

The shadow analogy

A 3-D object casts a 2-D shadow. Shine the light from a good angle and you can still recognise a hand or a teapot; from a bad angle it's an unreadable blob. Dimensionality reduction looks for the angle that keeps the most detail.

Why bother?

See it: plot 50-column data in 2-D. Speed: fewer columns train faster. Denoise: drop directions that are mostly noise. Fight the curse of dimensionality (below).

Selection vs extraction

Feature selection keeps some original columns and drops the rest. Feature extraction builds new columns as combinations of the old ones. PCA is extraction.

The main methods

PCA: linear, fast, the standard first choice. t-SNE / UMAP: non-linear, great for 2-D pictures of clusters, not for feeding models. Autoencoders: neural networks that learn a compressed code.

The curse of dimensionality, measured live

300 random points in a unit cube with d dimensions. For a random query point, we divide the distance to its nearest neighbour by the distance to its farthest. Near 0 means "near" and "far" are clearly different. Near 1 means everything is roughly equally far away, so distance-based methods (K-Means, KNN, DBSCAN) lose their footing.

Step 6 · Dimensionality reduction

Principal Component Analysis: find the direction of most spread

PCA finds new axes, called principal components, ordered by how much of the data's variation (spread) they capture. Keep the first few and drop the rest.

  1. Standardise each column (mean 0, standard deviation 1), so a column measured in thousands doesn't dominate.
  2. Compute the covariance matrix: how every pair of columns moves together.
  3. Its eigenvectors are the principal components (directions); its eigenvalues say how much variance each one holds. PC1 holds the most, PC2 the next, and each is at right angles to the ones before.
  4. Project each point onto the first k components. Those projections are your new, shorter columns.

Try it: rotate the line. Each point drops onto it (dashed). The spread of those shadows is the variance kept. PC1 is the angle where it's largest, which is also where the dashed lines are shortest in total (least information lost).

from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
Xs = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)
pca = PCA(n_components=0.95).fit(Xs)   # keep 95% of variance
pca.explained_variance_ratio_

The price of PCA: new columns are blends of old ones, so "PC2 = 0.8" is harder to explain than "Maths = 80". PCA only finds straight-line (linear) structure, and it's sensitive to scale, so always standardise first.
