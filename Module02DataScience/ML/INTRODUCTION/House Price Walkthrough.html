House Price Walkthrough

This page explains every line of a 76-line Python program that teaches a computer to estimate house prices. All numbers shown here are the real results of running the script.

The idea in one paragraph

We give the computer 16 example houses. For each one we know its area, bedrooms, age and the price it sold for. The computer studies 12 of them and works out a formula connecting the features to the price. We then check the formula on the 4 houses it didn't see, and finally use it to price a brand-new house.

Step 4 · Lines 43–45

  
Training the model

  

  
Two lines do all the learning:

  
    
    
  • model = LinearRegression() creates an empty, untrained model.
    • 
    
  • model.fit(X_train, y_train) is where learning happens. "Fit" means: find the formula that fits the 12 training houses best.
    • 
  

  
What formula is it learning?

  
Linear regression assumes the price is a weighted sum of the features:

  
price = intercept + (w₁ × area) + (w₂ × bedrooms) + (w₃ × age)

  
The numbers w₁, w₂, w₃ are called coefficients (weights) and the intercept is the starting value. Training chooses them so that the total squared error on the training houses is as small as possible. This method is called ordinary least squares.

  
Analogy: adjusting four knobs on a radio until the static (the error) is as quiet as possible. fit() turns the knobs for you in a fraction of a second.

Step 5 · Lines 47–51

  
Evaluating on the test houses

  

  
model.predict(X_test) asks the trained model to estimate prices for the 4 hidden houses. We then compare those guesses with the real prices.

  


  

    
How the two scores are calculated

    

    
Mean Absolute Error (MAE)

    

    
MAE is the average size of the mistake, in the same unit as the price. 1.85 means the model is off by about ₹1.85 lakh per house on average. Lower is better; 0 would be perfect.

    
R² score

    

    
R² compares the model with a lazy baseline that always guesses the average test price. 1.0 = perfect, 0 = no better than guessing the average, below 0 = worse than that. 0.99 means the model explains about 99% of the variation in these test prices.

    
Every house: actual vs predicted

    
    
Training houseTest houseDots on the diagonal line = perfect prediction

  

  
The f-string formatting

  
f"... {value:.2f} lakh" is an f-string: anything inside { } is calculated and inserted into the text. :.2f means "show as a decimal number with 2 digits after the point", so 1.845542… prints as 1.85.

  
Honest caveat about this score: with only 4 test houses, a high R² is encouraging but not proof. Real projects test on hundreds or thousands of examples.

Step 7 · Lines 65–72

  
Reading what the model learned

  

  

    
zip(X.columns, model.coef_)
zip pairs up two lists item by item: the first column name with the first coefficient, the second with the second, and so on. The for loop then prints each pair.

    
model.coef_  ·  model.intercept_
After fit(), scikit-learn stores the learned numbers in attributes ending with an underscore. coef_ holds w₁, w₂, w₃; intercept_ holds the starting value.

  

  

  
What each number means

  

    
area_sqft
+0.048
Each extra square foot adds about ₹0.048 lakh (₹4,823). So 100 extra sq ft ≈ +₹4.8 lakh, with bedrooms and age kept the same.

    
age_years
−0.208
Each extra year of age lowers the price by about ₹0.21 lakh (₹20,775). Older houses are worth a bit less, as expected.

    
bedrooms
−2.519
Surprising: more bedrooms lowers the price? See the explanation below.

    
Intercept
1.161
The formula's starting point: the "price" of a house with 0 sq ft, 0 bedrooms, 0 years. It has no real-world meaning; it just positions the line.

  

  
Why is the bedroom number negative?

  
Each coefficient means "the effect of this feature if all the others stay the same". If you keep the area fixed at 1500 sq ft and add a bedroom, each room has to get smaller, which buyers may value less. That's one possible reading.

  
The bigger reason is statistical: in this dataset, bedrooms rise almost exactly with area (bigger houses have more bedrooms). When two features move together, the model can't cleanly separate their effects, so their individual coefficients become unreliable. This is called multicollinearity. With only 12 training rows, it's easy to happen. The predictions are still good; only the individual coefficients shouldn't be over-interpreted.

  
You can check this in the Step 4 demo: turn off area_sqft and the bedrooms coefficient becomes strongly positive.
